# Purpose of This Document

This document translates the reference research and project vision into durable design principles for nattapong.dev. It should guide later decisions about content, hierarchy, visual language, interaction, and technical evidence, while preventing individual reference websites or short-lived trends from becoming the design itself.

It is a decision framework, not a final visual specification. It does not define a sitemap, homepage sequence, wireframe, typeface, palette, framework, or animation system. Later documents should use these principles to evaluate concrete options against the identity, audiences, and long-term purpose of the personal engineering website.

# Identity of nattapong.dev

nattapong.dev is a long-term personal engineering website and the central record of Nattapong's professional development. It should show not only completed work, but also how ideas are explored, systems are designed, decisions are made, knowledge is developed, and skills change over time.

The website represents an engineer through evidence and thought process. Projects matter, but they are part of a wider body of work that includes research, technical writing, experiments, learning notes, open-source activity, and professional background. Its value comes from the relationships among these materials: a project can produce a case study, a research question can produce notes and experiments, and unfinished work can later show progression.

Unlike a conventional portfolio, the website should not behave as a static gallery of polished outcomes or generic project cards. Unlike a SaaS landing page, it should not manufacture urgency, conversion pressure, customer scale, or a product ecosystem. Unlike an agency site, it should not make spectacle the main evidence of ability. Its central claim is personal and cumulative: this is what Nattapong builds, studies, learns, and understands, with enough context for visitors to judge the work honestly.

# Role of Each Reference

- **Apple:** Contributes disciplined focus. One idea should dominate at a time, overview content should remain concise, navigation should be predictable, and carefully prepared visual evidence should carry weight. The lesson is reduction and art direction, not Apple's promotional compositions, product language, typography, or interface styling.
- **Aristide Benoist:** Contributes controlled expressiveness. A stable grid can support irregular rhythm, strong scale changes, and moments of experimentation without making the experience incoherent. The lesson is to permit authored surprise where it strengthens identity, not to imitate any expressive composition or turn the site into a creative showcase.
- **Figma:** Contributes system thinking and demonstration. Reusable content structures can remain consistent while real interfaces, workflows, and artifacts make each subject distinct. The lesson is to show capability through the work and build for growth, not to copy Figma's colorful canvas language, product breadth, or mega-navigation.
- **Linear:** Contributes precision and process-based storytelling. Dense technical material becomes understandable when organized around a real workflow, introduced by concise explanations, and supported by interface states or progress evidence. The lesson is clarity and operational credibility, not Linear's dark palette, fine-line treatment, or software-product aesthetic.
- **Notion:** Contributes approachability and modular clarity. Complex capabilities can be explained through familiar situations, short writing, consistent content blocks, and evidence that remains understandable to non-specialists. The lesson is to make depth inviting, not to copy Notion's illustrations, cards, workspace terminology, or enterprise positioning.
- **Raycast:** Contributes behavioral proof and attention to engineering quality. Interaction, speed, reliability, extensibility, and workflows can be made tangible when demonstrations show meaningful actions. The lesson is to connect presentation to how a system behaves, not to reproduce launcher interfaces, keyboard motifs, glass effects, gradients, or cinematic darkness.
- **Stripe:** Contributes progressive technical explanation. Abstract systems can be made legible through connected diagrams, outcome-first summaries, verified measurements, and deeper developer material. The lesson is to connect architecture to consequences, not to borrow Stripe's gradients, financial metaphors, layered interface compositions, or claims of platform scale.
- **Vercel:** Contributes visible engineering structure. Architecture, deployment, status, constraints, and shipped changes can become concrete evidence rather than decorative technical signals. The lesson is to expose system relationships and current state, not to copy Vercel's monochrome grid, terminal panels, triangle motif, or infrastructure marketing language.

Each reference supplies a different method, not a visual ingredient. The final identity must emerge from Nattapong's content and voice rather than from combining their surfaces.

# Shared Principles Across the References

- **Show evidence instead of relying on claims.** Real interfaces, diagrams, workflows, results, and artifacts explain capability more credibly than abstract promotional language.
- **Give each section one clear responsibility.** A visitor should understand the subject, its importance, and the available next step without resolving competing messages.
- **Use progressive disclosure.** Provide an accessible summary first, then allow interested readers to reach technical depth without forcing every visitor through it.
- **Build a coherent system that can grow.** Reusable structures, consistent hierarchy, and stable interaction patterns should accommodate new work without making every page identical.
- **Separate spacious explanation from dense evidence.** Text needs calm reading conditions; interfaces, diagrams, and technical artifacts can carry more density when their purpose is clear.
- **Let content create visual variation.** Project interfaces, code, diagrams, photographs, and research artifacts should contribute character while the surrounding system maintains order.
- **Use motion to reveal behavior or relationships.** Animation earns its place when it demonstrates sequence, state, causality, or progress.
- **Keep navigation understandable.** Familiar labels and clear grouping outperform product-scale menus or experimental navigation when visitors have a concrete goal.
- **Preserve meaning across screen sizes.** Responsive design should prioritize the important state or message rather than merely shrinking desktop compositions.
- **Tie proof to context.** Metrics, screenshots, testimonials, logs, and technical terms are useful only when visitors can understand what they prove and under which conditions.

# Principles Specific to nattapong.dev

- The website should document development over time, not freeze Nattapong at one career stage.
- Engineering thinking is the primary differentiator. Explain requirements, constraints, alternatives, decisions, failures, and lessons alongside results.
- Finished and unfinished work should be clearly distinguished, with status treated as information rather than reputation management.
- Personal identity should come from genuine writing, selection, framing, and recurring ideas—not from a decorative personal brand applied over generic structures.
- Content must serve both rapid evaluation and sustained reading. A visitor should gain a reliable first impression quickly without sacrificing depth for those who continue.
- Technologies should appear in context as parts of systems and decisions, not as isolated badges or an exhaustive inventory of skills.
- The website should remain useful even when individual visual trends, tools, or areas of technical interest change.
- Research, notes, experiments, and learning are first-class evidence of engineering development, not secondary filler beneath completed projects.
- Maintenance is part of the design. New content should be addable without redesigning the site or weakening its hierarchy.
- Honest specificity is preferable to inflated polish. The website should never imply scale, maturity, collaboration, impact, or expertise that the underlying work does not establish.

# Audience Priorities

- **Recruiters and hiring teams:** Support fast, confident evaluation. They should be able to identify Nattapong's engineering direction, relevant capabilities, selected work, professional background, and contact path without reading every detail. Summaries should be concrete and scannable, with deeper evidence available when needed.
- **Engineers and technical readers:** Provide enough substance to assess how systems were built and how decisions were made. Architecture, constraints, code, tests, tradeoffs, and links to source material should be intelligible and connected to actual outcomes.
- **Lecturers and research collaborators:** Make research questions, methods, current status, findings, uncertainties, and related technical work easy to evaluate. Clearly distinguish established results from hypotheses, explorations, and future work.
- **Students and general visitors:** Offer plain-language entry points before specialized detail. Notes and explanations should teach without assuming familiarity, while optional depth should remain available rather than being removed.
- **Nattapong himself:** Act as a durable, maintainable record of growth. The structure should support revisiting earlier decisions, connecting related work, publishing incrementally, and seeing progression without rewriting history into a falsely polished narrative.

When audience needs conflict, use layered communication: concise outcome and context first, then technical evidence and extended explanation. No single audience should force the entire website into either shallow promotion or specialist-only documentation.

# Content Priorities

- **Work and engineering projects are the strongest proof of applied ability.** Feature fewer items with enough context to explain the problem, contribution, system, decisions, and result. Do not reduce them to screenshots or technology lists.
- **Research demonstrates disciplined inquiry.** Present questions, methods, evidence, findings, limitations, and current status. Research may be complete or ongoing, but its certainty must be explicit.
- **Technical notes form the long-term knowledge layer.** They should capture useful explanations, implementation discoveries, and reusable understanding. Their value is clarity and accuracy, not constant publication.
- **Experiments show curiosity and validation.** They may be smaller or less polished than projects, but should state what was tested, why it mattered, what happened, and whether further work is planned.
- **Learning progress makes development visible.** Record meaningful changes in understanding, milestones, and reflections rather than presenting an unfiltered activity stream or gamified streak.
- **Personal background supplies context and trust.** Include only information that helps visitors understand professional direction, interests, experience, or perspective; it should not overwhelm the engineering work.
- **Contact information completes the relationship.** It should be direct, current, accessible, and easy to find without adopting aggressive conversion patterns.

These roles define editorial importance, not a final sitemap or homepage order. The amount and prominence of each content type should be determined by the quality and maturity of the available material.

# Visual Character

The intended character is calm, precise, thoughtful, technically credible, and recognizably personal. It should feel crafted by an engineer who values clarity and understands visual communication, not generated from a fashionable template or styled as a commercial software company.

Restraint should create focus, not sterility. The foundation should be stable and timeless enough to support years of growth, while selected projects, research artifacts, diagrams, and authored motifs introduce variation. Visual confidence should come from hierarchy, proportion, editing, and evidence rather than excessive effects or luxury presentation.

The website may include moments of experimentation, but they should be local, content-driven, and understandable. The overall experience should remain professional and trustworthy. Neither uniform minimalism nor constant spectacle should erase the differences among projects and modes of work.

# Layout Principles

- Establish a dependable alignment system, then allow deliberate departures only when they clarify content or create a meaningful authored moment.
- Use a comfortable reading width for prose and broader regions for diagrams, interfaces, media, and comparisons. Do not force every content type into one measure.
- Create hierarchy through scale, placement, spacing, and evidence before adding decorative treatments.
- Give major ideas sufficient space, while keeping related technical details close enough to read as one system.
- Alternate calm explanatory regions with denser evidence regions to create rhythm without arbitrary layout changes.
- Prepare visual assets for their context. Crop, simplify, annotate, or provide responsive variants so screenshots and diagrams remain legible rather than becoming decorative texture.
- Preserve meaning on smaller screens by stacking, reordering, simplifying, or substituting visuals. Never shrink detailed desktop evidence below readable size.
- Avoid a uniform grid of generic cards. Reusable structures should support differences in importance, content type, maturity, and available evidence.
- Keep orientation clear on long pages through consistent headings, labels, metadata, and navigation cues.

# Typography Principles

Typography is the primary tool for making a content-rich engineering website approachable. It should feel clear, contemporary, and composed, with enough personality to support identity without competing with the work.

The hierarchy must distinguish page purpose, section ideas, project or note titles, body explanation, metadata, captions, and technical material at a glance. Display text should be concise; long-form text should use comfortable line length, spacing, and rhythm. Small labels must remain genuinely readable and must not be used merely to create a technical appearance.

Code, commands, output, measurements, and structured metadata may use a distinct typographic voice when that distinction improves comprehension. Technical typography should signal function, not decorate unrelated content. The system must support scanning, extended reading, responsive scaling, and consistent rendering without selecting a final typeface at this stage.

# Color Principles

Color should communicate hierarchy, state, category, relationship, and emphasis. A restrained foundation should keep prose readable and allow genuine project artifacts to retain their own character.

Accent color must have a consistent purpose. Interactive states should be distinguishable from decorative emphasis; status and diagram colors should remain interpretable beyond hue alone; and repeated domain or system-layer mappings should remain stable where they aid comprehension. Color may differentiate projects or content modes, but it should not fragment the website into unrelated themes.

Do not inherit any reference palette, treat gradients as automatic sophistication, or rely on low-contrast neutrals for atmosphere. Final values and light or dark foundations remain unresolved until real content and accessibility testing provide evidence.

# Motion and Interaction Principles

Motion is meaningful when it explains something that a static view cannot communicate as clearly: a workflow advancing, data moving through a system, an interface changing state, a test completing, a comparison revealing a difference, or an experiment producing a result.

Interaction should help visitors inspect evidence, move between overview and detail, or control a demonstration. Essential information and navigation must never depend on hover, autoplay, precise pointer movement, or successful animation. Controls should use familiar behavior, clear labels, visible focus, and appropriate touch targets.

Decorative motion should be rare, brief, and subordinate to reading. Avoid constant background movement, repeated scroll reveals, cinematic transitions, parallax used only for atmosphere, or simulated terminal activity with no evidentiary value.

Reduced-motion behavior is a designed state, not a blanket afterthought. When motion is decorative, remove it. When motion carries meaning, replace it with an immediate end state, a stepped transition, or a static sequence that preserves the same information. User control should be provided for media or demonstrations that continue, repeat, or require time to understand.

# Engineering Evidence

Engineering evidence should be selected and explained, not accumulated for visual effect.

- **Architecture:** Show the components and boundaries necessary to understand the system. Connect each part to a responsibility and keep diagrams proportionate to the project's real complexity.
- **Code:** Use short, relevant excerpts only when they illuminate a decision, interface, algorithm, or constraint. Provide context and, where available, a path to the fuller source.
- **Diagrams:** Explain data flow, dependencies, sequences, deployment, hardware relationships, or alternatives. Include labels and accompanying text so meaning is not encoded only visually.
- **Terminal output:** Include it only as proof of a meaningful operation such as building, testing, deploying, measuring, or reproducing a result. Curate noise without altering the truth of the output.
- **Benchmarks:** State what was measured, how, under which conditions, and against what baseline. Use only verified results and avoid implying broader performance than the evidence supports.
- **Constraints:** Name limits involving time, resources, devices, data, compatibility, scope, or knowledge when they shaped the work. Constraints help readers evaluate decisions fairly.
- **Tests:** Explain what behavior was checked and what confidence the tests provide. Counts and coverage values should not replace the meaning of the tested scenarios.
- **Design decisions:** Connect decisions to requirements or observations. Record alternatives when they materially explain why the chosen approach exists.
- **Tradeoffs:** State both the benefit and cost of important choices. Avoid presenting every decision as an unqualified success.
- **Results:** Separate completed outcomes, measured effects, observations, and expected benefits. Claims must match the available evidence.
- **Unfinished work:** Mark status clearly, explain what currently works, identify what is missing or uncertain, and note the next question when known. Do not disguise prototypes as production systems or erase abandoned paths that offer useful learning.

Evidence should be layered: a concise explanation for broad audiences, followed by optional artifacts and technical depth. Every artifact must answer a reader's likely question rather than merely signal that engineering occurred.

# Personal Identity and Originality

The strongest source of originality is the combination of Nattapong's actual work, questions, decisions, and way of explaining them. Writing should use a direct first-person voice where ownership, reflection, uncertainty, or learning matters. Avoid generic brand claims, inflated expertise, impersonal SaaS language, and interchangeable phrases about passion or innovation.

Project framing should reveal why a problem was worth attempting, what Nattapong was responsible for, what changed during the work, and what remains imperfect. Similar-looking outputs can become distinct through honest context and reasoning. Conversely, unique visuals cannot compensate for generic framing.

Visual motifs should be derived from recurring material in the work—such as structures, processes, annotations, measurements, or modes of documentation—only after enough real content exists to reveal a pattern. They should not be borrowed from reference brands or added as arbitrary developer decoration.

Experience should be represented at its actual level. Collaboration, scale, results, and technical ownership must be attributed accurately. Learning work can be valuable without being presented as expert or production work. This honesty, combined with consistent authorship over time, is what will make the website feel personal rather than AI-generated.

# Accessibility and Performance Principles

Accessibility and performance are non-negotiable qualities of the engineering work represented by the site.

- Use semantic structure, logical heading order, meaningful landmarks, descriptive links, and appropriate control semantics.
- Support full keyboard operation with predictable focus order and clearly visible focus states.
- Maintain sufficient contrast for text, controls, diagrams, borders that carry meaning, and all interaction states.
- Provide useful alternatives for screenshots, diagrams, demonstrations, video, audio, and animated sequences; do not embed essential explanations only inside images.
- Ensure layouts work with text enlargement, zoom, reflow, narrow screens, and differing input methods without losing information or functionality.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences and give users control over persistent or time-based media.
- Keep touch targets usable and avoid hover-only disclosure.
- Prioritize fast access to identity, selected work, reading content, and contact on realistic devices and networks.
- Treat images, fonts, scripts, and interactive demonstrations as performance costs that must justify themselves; load and render them in proportion to visitor need.
- Prevent visual effects from delaying content, shifting layout, blocking interaction, or making the site unusable when scripts or media fail.

Concrete budgets and testing targets should be established later, after the content model and technical approach are known. The absence of final numbers does not weaken the requirement that the site feel responsive and remain understandable under constrained conditions.

# Ideas to Keep

- A concise first layer followed by deeper project, research, and note content.
- Real interfaces, diagrams, code, and results as the primary visual evidence.
- Conventional, compact navigation with clear labels.
- Narrow reading measures paired with wider regions for technical visuals.
- Generous separation between major ideas and efficient density within evidence.
- Reusable content structures that can accept new material over time.
- Clear project metadata such as contribution, status, and context when supported.
- Outcome-led descriptions connected to the engineering decisions behind them.
- Verified measurements and concrete system states as credibility signals.
- Responsive art direction that preserves meaning rather than desktop geometry.
- Documentation, research, experiments, notes, and recent progress as first-class material.
- Purposeful motion for workflows, state changes, and system relationships.

# Ideas to Adapt

- **Single-message promotional sections:** Use their clarity for selected work, but replace sales claims with project context, contribution, evidence, and honest status.
- **Product-as-hero demonstrations:** Use real project behavior as evidence, but choose the artifact that best explains the work; not every project needs an application screenshot.
- **Reusable marketing modules:** Build consistent editorial structures, while allowing research, notes, experiments, and projects to use different evidence and levels of depth.
- **Large expressive typography:** Reserve it for a small number of important statements and keep it subordinate to readability and technical substance.
- **Irregular or experimental compositions:** Use them as occasional content-driven emphasis within a stable system, not as the default navigation or reading model.
- **Dark technical presentation:** Consider it only where it serves the material and maintains contrast; do not make darkness a substitute for engineering identity.
- **Visible grids and structural lines:** Use them to explain relationships, boundaries, or sequence, not as a copied brand texture across every surface.
- **Interface simulations:** Limit them to one meaningful behavior, keep them controllable and accessible, and provide a static explanation for visitors who cannot or choose not to interact.
- **Changelogs and recent activity:** Frame them as curated evidence of progression or shipped work, not an exhaustive feed or productivity score.
- **Social proof and statistics:** Replace corporate logo walls and scale claims with attributable collaboration, verified outcomes, repositories, tests, research evidence, or measured constraints.
- **Dual audience pathways:** Provide plain-language summaries and optional technical depth rather than separating visitors into complex product-style funnels.
- **Color from content:** Let artifacts contribute variation, but edit and frame them so the total experience remains coherent and accessible.
- **Technical command and terminal motifs:** Use actual, relevant output with explanation; never use fabricated commands as background decoration.
- **Project-specific art direction:** Allow selected work to have distinct visual emphasis while retaining common navigation, typography hierarchy, accessibility behavior, and editorial structure.

# Ideas to Reject

- Copying any reference's palette, typography, layout composition, illustration style, iconography, signature motif, or animation language.
- Combining recognizable surface elements from several references into a hybrid style.
- Treating the site as a funnel built around repeated conversion calls to action.
- Presenting every item as a generic card with a screenshot, tool list, and vague summary.
- Creating product-scale mega menus, ecosystem directories, or enterprise segmentation before the content requires them.
- Using oversized emptiness, cinematic media, gradients, glow, glass, or visible grids as automatic signs of quality.
- Making every project resemble a SaaS dashboard, cloud platform, command palette, financial system, or luxury product campaign.
- Using tiny labels, unreadable screenshots, raw terminal panels, or code fragments simply to look technical.
- Hiding important context behind interaction or moving media.
- Claiming scale, reliability, impact, collaboration, or completion without evidence.
- Centering AI in the identity unless the actual body of work makes it relevant, and using fashionable AI terminology without explanation.
- Sacrificing reading comfort, accessibility, performance, maintainability, or honesty for novelty.

# Conflicts Between References

- **Restraint versus visual experimentation:** Apple, Linear, Notion, and Vercel support reduction and consistency; Aristide Benoist and Figma demonstrate stronger visual variation. The current direction is restrained overall, with selective, content-driven experimentation that reveals personal authorship.
- **Conventional navigation versus exploratory navigation:** Apple, Linear, Notion, and Raycast favor predictable access, while more expressive approaches can encourage discovery. The current direction is conventional primary navigation with exploration occurring inside related content, not in the basic wayfinding model.
- **Light versus dark foundations:** Apple and several platform references use flexible or light foundations, while Linear and Raycast build much of their identity around darkness. This remains unresolved until representative prose, diagrams, screenshots, and accessibility prototypes are tested.
- **Static clarity versus cinematic motion:** Apple product storytelling and Raycast demonstrate the emotional and explanatory power of motion, while Notion, Linear, and Vercel show the value of controlled system behavior. The current direction favors static clarity by default and motion only when it communicates behavior, sequence, or causality.
- **Consistency versus project-specific art direction:** Figma and mature product systems show the strength of reusable patterns, while expressive references and Apple's campaigns show the value of subject-specific presentation. The current direction is a stable editorial and interaction system with bounded art direction for selected work.
- **Concise summaries versus deep technical explanation:** Every reference benefits from concise first-layer communication, while the project vision requires more technical depth than a marketing homepage. The direction is progressive disclosure: short, concrete summaries lead to substantial project, research, and note material.
- **Broad ecosystem visibility versus focused curation:** Figma, Notion, Stripe, and Vercel expose large ecosystems; Apple and Linear create stronger narrative prioritization. The direction is focused curation, expanding navigation or indexes only when the body of content makes them necessary.
- **Approachable language versus specialist precision:** Notion translates complexity into familiar situations, while Linear and Vercel assume technical fluency. The direction is plain-language framing with accurate terminology introduced where needed and supported by explanation.
- **Uniform visual identity versus artifact-led variety:** Linear and Vercel apply strong brand consistency, while Figma and Apple let showcased material create more variation. The direction is a restrained shared frame that allows authentic project and research artifacts to carry much of the variation.

# Open Design Questions

These decisions remain unresolved until real content, wireframes, prototypes, or technical constraints supply enough evidence:

- Which body of work best represents Nattapong's current engineering direction, and what deserves first-layer prominence?
- How much mature content exists in each mode—projects, research, notes, experiments, learning, and background—and what navigation depth does that volume justify?
- What distinction between project, case study, experiment, research item, and note is clear enough to remain useful as content grows?
- Which evidence is available for each featured work item, and which claims can be verified?
- What visual motifs emerge naturally from the actual artifacts and writing rather than from the reference sites?
- Should the primary foundation be light, dark, user-selectable, or context-dependent?
- How much visual variation can individual projects use before the website loses continuity?
- Which demonstrations genuinely need interaction or motion, and which communicate better as static sequences or diagrams?
- How should current progress and older work coexist without making the site feel like either an unfiltered feed or a frozen showcase?
- What level of first-person voice best balances personal reflection, technical precision, and professional clarity across different content types?
- Which responsive transformations are required by the real diagrams, screenshots, code, tables, and media?
- What performance budgets, content-loading strategy, and media constraints are appropriate once representative pages and assets exist?
- Which accessibility standards, testing methods, and content-authoring checks should become explicit acceptance criteria in later specifications?
- Which future features from the vision are supported by present needs, and which should remain deferred until real usage justifies them?

# Design DNA Summary

1. Build nattapong.dev as a living personal engineering record, not a static portfolio or commercial landing page.
2. Make Nattapong's thinking visible through problems, constraints, decisions, tradeoffs, evidence, and reflection.
3. Lead with concise, plain-language understanding, then provide optional technical depth.
4. Show real work and system behavior; never use technical artifacts as decoration.
5. Use a calm, durable design system that can grow without making every content type look the same.
6. Let authentic projects, research, and writing create personality and visual variation.
7. Keep navigation predictable and hierarchy strong so every audience can find an appropriate level of detail.
8. Use motion only to explain behavior, sequence, or change, with an equivalent reduced-motion experience.
9. Present finished, unfinished, successful, and uncertain work with accurate status and proportionate claims.
10. Treat readability, accessibility, performance, maintainability, and responsive meaning as non-negotiable engineering qualities.
11. Borrow principles from references, never their recognizable surface language.
12. Resolve visual choices through real content and prototypes, not trends or imagined project details.
